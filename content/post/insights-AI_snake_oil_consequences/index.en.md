We shouldn’t just ask what AI is capable of—we must also ask whether the results it produces are consistently correct. [*AI Snake Oil*](https://www.aisnakeoil.com/) argues that current AI overhype urgently needs **reliability engineering** to carry out systemic correction.

This essay further proposes: The challenge of AI reliability isn’t only technical—it deeply involves how power and responsibility are distributed in social-technical collaboration. More than blind trust, what deserves attention is the phenomenon of **manipulative responsibility evasion**.

## 🚨 Consequences of Overhype

AI—or in 2025, intelligent agents—are being overhyped with consequences that cannot be ignored. These systems not only “think” and “act”—they even promise to reshape entire industries. From sci-fi movies to automated legal tools, their projected vision is full of ambition.

But reality is saturated with unfulfilled promises.

Why do we keep mistaking product visions for actual achievements?

At a tech conference, Princeton PhD candidate in computer science **Sayash Kapoor** delivered a core insight: **AI hype must be reined in through reliability engineering.**

### 🤖 Capability ≠ Reliability

*AI Snake Oil* reminds us: What a model _**can do**_ (e.g. answer questions) and what it _**consistently does correctly**_ are two separate things.

- 🏆 High accuracy means “capable”: At least one correct answer appears across multiple outputs  
- 🔒 High reliability means “dependable”: Correct results every time, reaching engineering standards like “99.999% uptime”

A food-ordering assistant with 80% success rate isn’t impressive—it’s a disaster.

Engineering focus must shift from mere usability to verified **trustworthiness**, bridging that gap.

---

### 🧪 Intelligent Agents ≠ LLMs: Entirely Different Evaluation Logic

LLMs can be assessed with static benchmarks, such as single-call input/output accuracy.

But intelligent agents operate in **dynamic environments**, spawning sub-agents, calling APIs, and maintaining state across complex task chains.

Thus, agent evaluation must include:

- ⛱ Sandbox tests: Simulating edge-case scenarios in the real world  
- ⏳ Multi-step workflows, not one-shot metrics  
- 🔄 Continuous monitoring—not just one-time accuracy testing  

If the system gives not **verifiable answers**, but **educated guesses**—it poses ethical concerns.

## 🧱 Reliability Engineering as a Social Challenge

*AI Snake Oil* draws on principles from engineering’s reliability discipline, especially for AI used in high-risk sectors like healthcare, hiring, and criminal justice.

It warns: Many systems fall short of reliability standards—yet are widely adopted or implicitly trusted.

Which raises the question: **Are we trusting engineering outcomes—or just persuasive marketing?**

## ✏️ Han-Teng’s Comment

Since the 1990s, globalization and internet technologies have made societies increasingly complex. Humans now rely on ever more “socio-technical” solutions—and traditional mechanisms of trust are struggling to keep up.

I argue: This is not just a tech problem—it’s a **trust crisis rooted in social division of labor.**

---

### 🙈🙉🙊 Reconstructing Trust in a Divided Society

As AI enters hiring, healthcare, lending and other domains, it exposes how specialized knowledge and responsibility are **shouldered**, **fragmented**, or even **dodged**:

- Can medical records be used as hiring criteria?  
- Should health history determine insurance premiums?  
- Is it valid to judge loan eligibility based on criminal records?

*AI Snake Oil* uses the metaphor “snake oil” to reflect humanity’s deep anxiety about uncertainty. Historically, we built systems like religion, divination, medicine to cope—are we now using AI to redistribute or conceal responsibility?

---

### 🕊️ Luhmann’s Framework for Trust

Social theorist **Niklas Luhmann** argued: **Trust is a mechanism to simplify complexity.**

In uncertain environments, trust enables collaboration—without requiring full visibility into all risks.

If AI systems cross boundaries between law, healthcare, finance, and other social subsystems, we must ask:

Have they built meaningful trust between those domains—or merely shifted responsibility via data pipelines?

---

### ⛑ Plausible Deniability: A New Norm of Blurred Accountability

More troubling than blind trust is what lurks in AI’s architecture of “**plausible deniability**”:

- “It’s not us rejecting you—our system flagged you as unsuitable.”  
- “The premium hike isn’t our call—it’s a model’s risk assessment.”  
- “Your loan was denied not by us—but by the predictive algorithm.”

This is strategic boundary manipulation—using ambiguity across systems to evade accountability.

## 🗺️ Geopolitical Considerations
In a geopolitical landscape where China prioritizes internal stability and the United States grapples with risk society both at home and abroad, the deployment of AI systems reflects deeper asymmetries in institutional trust and governance logic. 

When intelligent agents blur traditional boundaries between legal, financial, medical and even intelligence communities, systems risk becoming instruments of “plausible deniability.” Niklas Luhmann’s theory reminds us that trust is socially constructed—and fragile. 

While China may encode AI deployment within tightly controlled institutional hierarchies, the U.S. faces the challenge of ensuring transparency in more decentralized and pluralistic environments. In both contexts, if intelligent systems reinforce risk aversion without clarifying accountability, AI becomes not a stabilizing force, but a new source of systemic uncertainty and disruptions.

## ⚡ Four Questions Worth Pursuing

- 🧱 Why do intelligent agents enter collaborative domains at all? What trust systems are activated?  
- 🗂️ Are they replacing collaboration—or enhancing it?  
- 🧑‍💼 Do agents alter the balance of power and responsibility? Is more power matched by less accountability?  
- 👷‍♂ Can engineering—especially reliability engineering—learn from social sciences (e.g. systems theory, risk sociology) to implement **High Reliability Organization (HRO)** principles for AI systems?

**The central challenge of AI reliability engineering lies not only in technical verifiability, but in whether responsibility across social subsystems remains legitimately distributed.**
